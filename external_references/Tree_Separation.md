Optimal Pipeline Design for Understory Separation and Individual Tree Segmentation in Terrestrial Laser Scanning
Executive Summary
The processing of Terrestrial Laser Scanning (TLS) point clouds in complex forest ecosystems presents a profound geometric, spatial, and topological challenge. Extracting accurate dendrometric parameters—such as Diameter at Breast Height (DBH), Tree Height (TH), and Above Ground Biomass (AGB)—requires the precise isolation of individual tree structures. This encompasses the stem, primary branches, and upper canopy, while rigorously suppressing false positives generated by dense understory vegetation, climbing vines, shrubs, and suppressed saplings 1. This challenge is particularly acute in pine-dominant plots characterized by extensive lower-stratum regeneration and overlapping crowns, where classical heuristic assumptions regarding local spatial connectivity frequently fail. Understory vegetation mimics the geometric signatures of fine woody material and creates artificial topological bridges between adjacent trees, leading to severe under-segmentation and the merging of distinct tree instances 21.
The analysis synthesizes recent advancements in graph-based clustering, multi-scale geometric feature engineering, and trunk-connectivity constraints to construct a robust, production-ready pipeline architecture. A paradigm shift is currently occurring in the field: researchers are moving away from purely local, bottom-up region-growing algorithms—which are highly susceptible to occlusion and crown intersection—toward global graph-cut, shortest-path optimization algorithms, and deep-learning semantic masks 34. By integrating localized, slice-based trunk detection with global topological reasoning, it is possible to enforce strict structural connectivity from the uppermost canopy back to the root system, thereby severing erroneous understory linkages and filtering out floating artifacts 5.
The proposed pipeline blueprint leverages a specialized "stripe" or "high-slice" spatial constraint strategy to completely bypass the densest understory layers, establishing highly reliable trunk seeds from which the rest of the tree can be mathematically grown 6. From these verified stem seeds, a multi-scale graph is constructed using anisotropic edge weighting and Dijkstra-based shortest-path traversals to accurately delineate the canopy 5. Simultaneously, a specialized top-level correction mechanism protects the upper canopy strata from fragmentation, ensuring that legitimate, sparsely populated crown points are not falsely pruned as understory noise 4. This report extensively details the mechanics of connected component strategies, eigenvalue-derived geometric filtering, and per-component validation rules, culminating in a highly structured, scalable implementation roadmap and ablation protocol designed for immediate deployment in precision forestry.
1. Structured Literature Review (2018–Present)
The evolution of Individual Tree Segmentation (ITS) and understory vegetation separation in TLS data has progressed through three distinct algorithmic generations: heuristic region-growing, mathematically rigorous graph-partitioning, and deep learning architectures. Understanding this evolutionary trajectory is critical for identifying the limitations of legacy systems and the strengths of modern topological approaches.
Early robust efforts heavily utilized bottom-up region growing and classical cylinder fitting. The treeseg algorithm, developed by Burt et al. (2019), exemplifies this era. It utilizes Euclidean clustering, normal-based region growing, and Random Sample Consensus (RANSAC) cylinder fitting to iteratively build stems from the ground up, followed by connectivity-based crown allocation 74. While highly effective in sparse or heavily managed plantations, such approaches exhibit severe computational bottlenecks and structural failures in natural forests where crown intersections and dense understories violate the assumption of distinct spatial gaps. Region-growing algorithms often "leak" into the understory, classifying dense continuous shrubbery as part of the main tree bole 4.
To overcome these localized limitations, the field rapidly adopted graph-based global optimization. Algorithms like TLSeparation (Vicari et al., 2019) introduced the concept of eigenvalue thresholds combined with shortest-path Euclidean metrics to distinguish the chaotic distributions of leaves and understory from the highly structured, linear profiles of woody components 89. The LeWoS (Leaf-Wood Separation) algorithm by Wang et al. (2020) advanced this by utilizing "chaos distance" and region-growing on point clusters, which proved superior to purely localized pointwise classification 10. This was further refined by Shen et al. into the Node Expansion-Path Concatenation (NE-PC) model, which expands nodes with similar curvature and verticality into "wood nodes" to prevent the omission of trunk points during path frequency detection, significantly improving the preservation of fine branches 11.
The treeiso algorithm, introduced by Xi and Hopkinson (2022), represents a pivotal advancement in instance segmentation by reframing the point cloud as a connected graph and utilizing the  cut-pursuit algorithm to isolate instances 12. By implementing a hierarchical local-to-global clustering mechanism, treeiso aggregates small clusters into major segments. Crucially, it utilizes a specialized elevation-difference-to-length ratio () to execute top-level corrections 4. This top-level constraint effectively mitigates the over-segmentation of complex crowns while rejecting disconnected understory noise.
Simultaneously, tools prioritizing operational accessibility, such as 3DFin (Laino et al., 2024), have optimized heuristic approaches through highly parameterized Graphical User Interfaces (GUIs) 613. The 3DFin pipeline introduced the highly effective "stripe" methodology, which identifies stems within a user-defined horizontal elevation range (e.g., 1.5 m to 4.5 m) specifically chosen to bypass ground-level shrubs and grasses 62. It employs iterative pruning intensities to mechanically strip away fine branches and attached understory, achieving remarkable processing speeds while maintaining high DBH estimation accuracy 13.
Most recently, hybrid rule-based and machine learning (ML) approaches have established new state-of-the-art benchmarks. The TLS2trees pipeline (Wilkes et al., 2023) integrates deep-learning-powered semantic segmentation (via Sparse 3D Convolutional Neural Networks like FSCT) to definitively classify wood versus leaf/understory points before passing the filtered wood points into a graph-based instance segmentation workflow 5. By stripping the understory out at the semantic level, the subsequent topological separation of individual stems via Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise (DBSCAN) and Dijkstra shortest-path algorithms becomes drastically simplified and mathematically stable 5.
2. Taxonomy of Point Cloud Segmentation Methods
The separation of understory from the true tree structure is achieved through algorithms that can be categorized into four primary taxonomic branches. Each branch encodes distinct assumptions regarding the physical manifestation of forest topology, point density, and spatial continuity.
2.1 Graph and Connected Components (CC) Methods
Graph-based approaches convert the unstructured 3D point cloud into a mathematical topological network. Nodes represent either individual points, downsampled voxels, or supervoxels (localized clusters of points exhibiting similar geometric normals). Edges are drawn between nodes based on k-Nearest Neighbors (kNN) or fixed-radius spherical neighborhoods. To separate understory from trees, the edge weights are formulated to heavily penalize unnatural structural transitions 5.
A standard spatial CC approach—where any points within a Euclidean distance  are grouped—fails catastrophically in dense forests. Understory vegetation physically touches both the ground and the lower branches, causing adjacent trees to fuse into unresolvable super-clusters 2. To counter this, advanced CC pipelines employ anisotropic, weighted graphs. The edge weight function, , incorporates not only Euclidean distance but also the dot product of the normal vectors between node  and node . If two supervoxels have normal vectors that point in radically different directions—a hallmark of chaotic understory leaves—or if the transition requires a sharp horizontal jump across a gap, the edge weight approaches infinity.
Minimum graph-cut algorithms sever the weakest connections in this network, which typically occur at the physical interface where a thin understory branch touches a main trunk. Alternatively, shortest-path algorithms (e.g., Dijkstra) trace the most direct, lowest-cost route from a canopy node back to a designated ground-level root node; if a piece of vegetation lacks a direct, low-cost path to a thick, vertical root structure, it is aggressively pruned as understory 3.

Beyond eigenvalues, higher-order mathematical surface descriptors such as Curvature and Roughness are deployed.
Curvature: By fitting a local quadric surface to the point neighborhood, both Mean Curvature () and Gaussian Curvature () can be extracted. A mature tree trunk geometrically approximates a cylinder; therefore, its Gaussian Curvature approaches zero () while its Mean Curvature remains non-zero. Conversely, complex understory bushes exhibit highly varying, non-zero Gaussian curvature across very short spatial distances.
Roughness: Quantified as the standard deviation of point distances to the best-fit local plane or cylinder 14. Understory vegetation produces exceptionally high roughness values due to varied leaf angles and overlapping, intersecting shrubs. A stringent maximum roughness threshold ensures that only the relatively continuous surface of a tree trunk is retained during the stem-seeding phase 14.
Density Metrics: The spatial density of points varies fundamentally between wood and leaves. Surface density (points per square meter on a fitted plane) is exceptionally high on a solid tree trunk. However, volume density (voxel occupancy counts) is lower in the understory because leaves create occlusion shells with hollow interiors. Measuring the neighbor count within a tight search sphere rapidly isolates solid wood surfaces from scattered vegetation.

Point-based neural networks (e.g., PointNet++, SparseConvNet, or SOUL) are trained to predict a discrete semantic probability score for every point 1516.

This architecture effectively isolates the complex semantic decision-making to the deep neural network, leaving the deterministic spatial grouping to the highly efficient rule-based algorithms 5.
2.4 Forest-Specialised Pipelines and Allometric Constraints
These pipelines bypass generic point-cloud processing and encode hard ecological and dendrometric constraints directly into the segmentation logic. The 3DFin software represents the pinnacle of this approach. It forces the algorithm to look for initial stems exclusively within a specific vertical "stripe" 62. By mathematically locking the search space to an elevation range (e.g., exactly 2.0 m to 4.0 m above the normalized digital terrain model), the software guarantees that dense ground-level understory is structurally invisible to the seeding algorithm.
Furthermore, these pipelines apply expected allometric scaling rules via "Expert" parameters. For example, a segmented vertical component might have its bounding box evaluated; if the ratio of the component's DBH to its height is impossibly high, or if its Expected maximum diameter falls below a minimal operational threshold (e.g., 0.1 m), the entire component is immediately deleted as an understory bush 172.

4. Dendometrics, Occlusion, and Robustness to Scan Configurations
The physical interaction between the TLS laser pulse, the tree morphology, and the scan geometry dictates the quality of the derived dendrometrics. A robust understory pipeline must explicitly account for these physical limitations to prevent data corruption.
When extracting DBH, Tree Height (TH), and calculating volume or Above Ground Biomass (AGB) via Quantitative Structure Models (QSMs), the presence of unremoved understory or the effects of scan occlusion create severe structural hallucinations 1. Leaf and understory points that are mistakenly classified as wood emulate solid components, forcing the QSM cylinder-fitting algorithms to generate massive, artificial branching structures where none physically exist 1. This radically overestimates biomass. Conversely, occlusion—where parts of the stem or branches are shadowed from the scanner by dense mid-story foliage or large adjacent trunks—creates massive spatial gaps in the point cloud 1. If the graph connectivity thresholds are too strict, these occlusion gaps will cause the shortest-path algorithm to sever the tree in half, resulting in severe TH underestimation and orphaned canopies.
Furthermore, beam divergence at extended ranges causes the laser footprint to expand, leading to the systematic overestimation of small branch diameters 1. When deploying in dense, unmanaged plots with varying species, single-scan setups are inadequate. Multi-station scanning is mandatory to minimize shadow effects, though this introduces registration noise and exacerbates the blurring caused by wind sway in the upper canopy. A highly tuned pipeline mitigates this by applying data quality adjustments, lowering spatial rigidness thresholds in the upper strata where wind sway is maximal, and utilizing relative distance-to-ground metrics rather than absolute Z-coordinates to handle sloping terrain.
5. Deep-Dive: Core Technical Mechanics
To design a robust segmentation pipeline that actively discriminates between an irregular pine trunk and a dense Rubus understory bush, it is essential to deeply understand the underlying mathematical operations. The separation relies on a delicate orchestration of spatial slicing, connectivity enforcement, and iterative component pruning.
5.1 Trunk-First Seeding and Connectivity Enforcement
Attempting to segment the entire tree simultaneously—processing leaves, branches, and trunks at once—is highly error-prone in dense environments. The most robust methodologies operate strictly on a trunk-first paradigm. Because the trunk is the most geometrically distinct, continuous, and voluminous feature of the tree, it must be isolated first to serve as the unshakeable structural anchor for the graph.
In pine-dominant plots (e.g., Pinus sylvestris), the lower branches are often dead and shed as the canopy closes, creating a clean, distinct lower bole. However, a dense secondary understory typically surrounds the absolute base (0m to 1.5m). To exploit this biological morphology, the pipeline isolates a horizontal "stripe" of the point cloud—for instance, strictly between 1.5 m and 4.0 m above the normalized DTM. Within this slice, the ground has been mathematically eliminated, and the sprawling upper canopy has not yet begun. Only the clean boles of the trees and exceptionally tall understory remain 62.
Within this isolated stripe, 2D circular Hough Transforms or density-based clustering (DBSCAN) are applied 3. Points that successfully form dense, highly vertical cylinders are locked in as verified "Trunk Seeds." Trunk-connectivity enforcement then dictates an absolute rule: no subsequent cluster of points in the canopy or mid-story can be classified as a valid tree instance unless a continuous, low-cost topological path can be traced back to one of these verified Trunk Seeds. If a dense pocket of vegetation in the mid-story lacks a path to a seed, or if the path must cross an impossibly horizontal gap, the cluster is definitively flagged as disconnected understory noise and deleted.
5.2 Canopy Protection via Slice-Based Constraints (High-Slice Strategy)
A major risk in aggressive understory removal and gap-based graph segmentation is canopy fragmentation—where the legitimate upper branches of a tree, which are thin, widely dispersed, and heavily swayed by wind, are falsely identified as understory noise and deleted.
To prevent this catastrophic loss of dendrometric volume, pipelines deploy a "High-Slice" or "Top-Level Correction" strategy. As demonstrated in the treeiso architecture, the point cloud is analyzed in horizontal strata from bottom to top. The algorithm calculates the  ratio for spatial segments, defined as the elevation difference of the segment divided by its total 3D length 4. The main trunk has a  near 1.0 (purely vertical). As the algorithm moves up into the high slices of the canopy, branches become increasingly horizontal, and  drops significantly.
By dynamically adjusting the geometric thresholds in the upper slices—specifically above —the algorithm protects the canopy 4. It permits lower verticality, higher sphericity, and wider spatial gaps for points located high above the ground. To resolve isolated fragments, it utilizes a -nearest neighbor voting system () to forcefully fuse disconnected upper-canopy clusters back to the main tree structure 4. This top-level correction preserves the delicate crown architecture while maintaining the strict, uncompromising filters at the bottom of the tree to eliminate the understory.
5.3 Component-Level Pruning and Validation Rules
Once the initial connected components are formed and assigned to Trunk Seeds, the pipeline performs a rigorous forensic analysis on each isolated object. The 3DFin software logic utilizes an iterative "Pruning Intensity" variable to clean the object 62.
A pruning intensity setting of 2 or 3 forces the algorithm to iteratively strip the outermost boundary points of a detected component. If an object is a false positive caused by a dense, leafy bush, iterative pruning will rapidly dissolve the entire object into nothing, as it lacks a dense, solid wood core. Conversely, if the object is a true tree trunk, the pruning will merely strip away the attached understory twigs, vines, and loose bark, leaving the massive, high-density cylindrical wood core perfectly intact 2.
Furthermore, "Expert" bounding-box parameters are strictly enforced. If a segmented component has dimensions that wildly violate local pine allometric scaling (e.g., a component that is 4 meters wide but only 1.2 meters tall), it is immediately flagged as understory scrub and deleted 172.
6. Recommended Pipeline Blueprint for TLS Understory Separation
Synthesizing the state-of-the-art across graph connectivity, spatial constraints, and multi-scale eigenvalue rules, the following ordered pipeline blueprint is recommended for achieving minimal false positives and false negatives, specifically adapted for pine-dominant plots with dense understory.
Stage 1: Absolute Ground Normalization
Input: Raw Multi-Station TLS Point Cloud (e.g., .las, .laz, or .ply).
Action: Apply the Cloth Simulation Filter (CSF) to mathematically extract the Digital Terrain Model (DTM). Compute the vertical distance of every point to the nearest DTM facet to create a normalized point cloud where the Z-axis represents true height above ground, independent of topography.
Critical Parameter: Cloth Resolution. Set default to 0.4 m. If set too high (e.g., 0.7 m), the simulated cloth will bridge over dense ground shrubs instead of slipping between them, causing the DTM to float and resulting in the lowest sections of the trunks being permanently clipped 2.
Stage 2: The "Clean Stripe" Trunk Seeding (3DFin Logic)
Input: Normalized Point Cloud.
Action: Horizontally slice the point cloud to isolate the specific elevation region where pine trunks are thickest and understory interference is biologically minimized. Apply density-based clustering (DBSCAN) exclusively within this slice.
Configuration (via config.ini equivalent):
lower_limit: 1.5 m (clears dense ground herbs, ferns, and low shrubs).
upper_limit: 4.5 m (stops before the primary pine branching begins) 62.
Stem search diameter: 0.5 m 2.
DBSCAN_eps: 0.1 m.
DBSCAN_min_points: 100 (density filter to instantly reject sparse understory leaves).
Output: A set of highly verified, robust trunk base centroids acting as Root Nodes.
Stage 3: Multi-Scale Geometric Wood Filtering
Input: All points outside the Clean Stripe.
Action: Compute covariance matrices at multiple radii (r = 0.1m, 0.2m, 0.5m). Calculate Sphericity (), Linearity (), and Gaussian Curvature ().

Delete points where Sphericity  (aggressively removes chaotic understory foliage).
Retain points where Feature 5 > -0.9 99.
Output: A semantically masked point cloud heavily biased toward rigid woody structures. The understory density is structurally devastated, simplifying the upcoming graph traversal.
Stage 4: Dijkstra Graph Construction & Shortest Path Allocation
Input: Filtered Point Cloud & Verified Trunk Seeds.
Action: Construct an anisotropic spatial graph. Edge weights between point clusters are defined by Euclidean distance multiplied by the angular deviation penalty. Establish the Trunk Seeds as the root nodes. Run the Dijkstra shortest-path algorithm. Every point is assigned to the Tree ID of the Trunk Seed it can reach with the lowest cumulative topological cost.
Decision Logic (Trunk-Connection): If the shortest path cost for a cluster of points exceeds a predefined Max_Path_Cost_Threshold, the path is deemed structurally invalid. The cluster is declared to be disconnected understory and permanently deleted 5.

Input: Assigned Tree Instances (including initial canopy fragments).

7. Evaluation Protocol and CloudCompare Validation
To objectively quantify the success of the understory separation and tree preservation pipeline, a rigorous evaluation protocol must be established, utilizing both automated quantitative benchmarks and interactive visual validation.
Quantitative Benchmarks
Based on established benchmarking frameworks for forest TLS (such as the UCL benchmark by Wilkes et al.), evaluation must be executed at two distinct levels using Intersection over Union (IoU) as the primary mathematical matching criterion 16. A predicted tree is only considered a True Positive (TP) match to a ground-truth tree if their mutual IoU exceeds 0.5.
Plot-Level Metrics (Tree Preservation vs. Omission):
Precision: Computed as . Measures how many segmented objects are true trees. A low precision indicates that large understory shrubs are surviving the geometric filters and being falsely classified as distinct trees (False Positives).
Recall: Computed as . Measures how many ground-truth trees were found. A low recall indicates that the geometric filters or graph-cuts were too aggressive, deleting legitimate trees alongside the understory (False Negatives).
F1-Score: The harmonic mean of plot precision and recall, serving as the ultimate indicator of overall plot-level success 16.
Instance-Level Metrics (Understory Removal Accuracy):
mPrec (Mean Precision): The fraction of points in the segmented tree that belong to the true tree. High mPrec proves the understory attached to the trunk was successfully sheared off without being absorbed into the tree model.
mRec (Mean Recall): The fraction of ground truth points successfully captured in the final model. High mRec proves the top-level canopy protection successfully prevented the crown from being pruned away 16.
Validation Workflow in CloudCompare
Quantitative metrics require meticulously annotated, point-by-point ground-truth data, which is highly labor-intensive. For real-world operational validation, an interactive workflow utilizing the open-source software CloudCompare (version 2.13 or higher) is mandatory 2122.
Ingestion & DTM Check: Import the pipeline's output (individual point clouds per tree) alongside the raw plot scan. Utilize CloudCompare's Cross Section tool to take a 0.5m deep horizontal slice across the entire plot. Visually verify that the CSF generated a DTM that perfectly hugs the ground beneath the understory; floating DTMs will ruin the normalization 2.
Local Statistical Testing: For ambiguous trees suspected of being massive understory bushes, apply CloudCompare's Tools > Statistics > Compute Stat Params 23. Verify the local point density and curvature. If a segmented object exhibits wild density variations inconsistent with a solid cylindrical trunk, manual deletion is executed 23.
Interactive Segmentation: Use the manual segment (polygon clipping) tool to snip away residual understory artifacts that survived the pruning stage at the trunk base 21. Document the volume and frequency of these manual corrections to iteratively tune the pipeline's Pruning Intensity and number_of_iterations parameters 21.
8. Risk Register: Error Modes and Mitigation
No purely algorithmic pipeline is infallible. The physical complexity of a biological forest guarantees edge-case geometric failures. Identifying these likely failure modes and establishing proactive fallback strategies is critical for robust, industrial-scale deployment.

Root Cause

Stem Erosion at Lower Levels
High Pruning Intensity combined with low point density causes the iterative boundary deletion algorithm to strip away actual bark points, leading to a fragmented, hourglass-shaped trunk base 6.
Reduce Pruning Intensity to 1. Implement a localized mathematical cylinder-lock: if RANSAC identifies a perfect cylinder with high inlier density, freeze those points from any further iterative pruning.
Attached Understory (Vines/Epiphytes)
Vines wrap tightly around the trunk, perfectly matching its verticality and linearity. The graph shortest-path algorithm treats them as part of the stem surface 1.
Utilize laser intensity/reflectance thresholding. Leaves and vine tissues often possess different near-infrared reflectance signatures compared to mature, dry pine bark. Filter by intensity prior to graph construction.
Canopy Fragmentation
The Dijkstra path cost exceeds the strict threshold at primary branch intersections where the geometric angle changes sharply, completely severing the crown from the stem 5.
Enhance the Top-Level Correction. Relax the angular penalty in the graph weights for any points located above 10m on the normalized Z-axis, explicitly prioritizing connectivity over straightness in the upper strata 4.

Large pine trunks block the laser, leaving a widening "shadow" cone behind them. Understory in this shadow is not scanned, but the backside of a neighboring tree is also lost, mimicking a spatial gap 1.

9. Implementation Roadmap
To transition this theoretical research into a production-ready, highly automated system, a phased implementation roadmap is required, escalating in algorithmic complexity.
Phase 1: Quick-Win Baseline (Rule-Based + Heuristic)
Objective: Rapid deployment for standard, relatively well-managed pine plantations requiring immediate biomass estimation.
Architecture: Leverage the existing 3DFin open-source architecture via its Command Line Interface (CLI) for batch processing 6.
Action: Configure the 3DFinconfig.ini file accurately. Set the lower_limit to explicitly bypass the localized average height of the understory. Activate the Clean noise on DTM function 2.
Result: Fast (2-7 minutes per plot), computationally light extraction of 80%+ of the dominant pine trees 13. However, it possesses limited capability in handling severely intersecting crowns or complex branching.
Phase 2: Improved Hybrid Version (Graph-Based Topological Cut)
Objective: Handle dense, natural unmanaged plots with overlapping crowns and severe understory physical bridging.
Architecture: Build a custom Python pipeline wrapping PDAL (for CSF ground filtering) and NetworkX (for Dijkstra graph operations) 5.
Action: Implement the Multi-Scale Geometric Wood Filtering (eigenvalue and curvature extraction). Construct the anisotropic weighted graph. Use the 3DFin stripe method merely to identify root node coordinates, then hand control to the graph-cut algorithm to define the actual complex 3D instance boundaries 5.
Result: Significant, measurable reduction in under-segmentation. Requires notably higher computational overhead (RAM intensive for large point-graphs).
Phase 3: Advanced Version (Deep Learning for Ambiguous Regions)
Objective: Achieve State-of-the-Art (SOTA) accuracy with near-zero false positives, perfectly isolating the dendrometrics regardless of understory density.
Architecture: Integrate the FSCT (Forest Structure Complexity Tool) or TreeLearn 3D Convolutional Neural Network architectures directly into the ingestion phase 516.
Action: Pre-process the plot through the trained neural network to generate semantic labels (Wood vs. Leaf vs. Ground vs. Understory). Delete all non-wood points. Feed the remaining structurally clean, heavily thinned point cloud into the Phase 2 Graph-Based algorithm to perform the final instance separation 5.
Result: Absolute structural robustness to species variation and impenetrable understory density. Computationally demanding; requires high-end CUDA-capable GPUs for efficient neural network inference.

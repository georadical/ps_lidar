# Stem straightness, sweep, curvature and crook from LiDAR point clouds: literature and algorithms (state as of Nov 2026)

Scope note: These notes are written so they can be compared with the PS_LiDAR pipeline (CSF, then height normalisation, then pgeof+DBSCAN stem detection, then ellipse fits every 0.2 m, then vertical tracking, then a centerline polyline, then sliding-window chord-deviation sweep classes 8/L/S/3/1/X/W/K with SED-fraction thresholds, n_bows, and a 15° turn-angle kink test). Items marked **[unverified]** come only from search snippets or secondary summaries. I could not read the primary text for them.

Terminology warning that matters for the comparison: in the Finnish/Aalto/FGI literature (Liang, Kankare, Hyyppä, Yrttimaa, Muhojoki), **"stem curve" means the diameter-vs-height profile (taper curve), not the curvature of the stem axis**. Their stem-curve RMSEs are diameter errors, not centerline/sweep errors. Very few papers validate the *axis shape* (sweep, crook, sinuosity) against an independent reference.

## Q1. Which papers estimate stem curvature / sweep / straightness / crook from LiDAR (or from 3D log scanners)?

### Takeaway
There is a small validated literature on the shape of the stem axis. Seminal: Thies & Pfeifer 2004 (TLS). Key recent papers: Prendes et al. 2022 (TLS, *P. pinaster*), Gorges et al. 2021 (sawmill 3D log scanner, five curvature algorithms), Pyörälä et al. 2019 (TLS vs sawmill, Scots pine; sweep was the worst attribute), and de Paula Pires et al. 2026 (TLS crook detection with YOLO). Applied Australian/NZ work is mostly FWPA projects on helicopter ULS/ALS of *P. radiata*, done with human VR cruising or deep-learning stem models. None of it validates automated sweep codes against field cruisers. I found no peer-reviewed paper that converts LiDAR centerlines into Interpine/HQP-style sweep codes and validates them.

### Cited Findings

**Seminal / older**
- Thies & Pfeifer (2004), "Three-dimensional reconstruction of stems for assessment of taper, sweep and lean based on laser scanning of standing trees", *Scand. J. For. Res.* 19(6):571–581. Method: filter the TLS point cloud, then fit a parametric stem model, then derive DBH, crown base, taper, sweep and lean. Numeric accuracy for sweep **[unverified, full text not accessed]** — [TU Delft record](https://repository.tudelft.nl/record/uuid:a5e8f7bf-35fa-4fc4-8656-25b6f6025cf4); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/02827580410019562)
- UK Forestry Commission Technical Note FCTN021 (Price, Hapca, Gardiner, Macdonald, McLean, July 2017). It compares visual scoring, two-view photogrammetry and TLS for straightness of standing conifers (developed for Sitka spruce). TLS approach: "geometrically fit a sequence of cylinders joined tip to tail" to derive taper and straightness. Photogrammetry measures the lower 10–12 m. It measures stem diameter and orientation "to within 7 mm" with a 3-MP camera and also yields lean angle, sinuosity and lean orientation — [Forest Research TN021 PDF](https://cdn.forestresearch.gov.uk/2022/04/Assessing-the-stem-straightness-of-trees.pdf)
- Liang, Kankare, Yu, Hyyppä & Holopainen (2014), "Automated Stem Curve Measurement Using Terrestrial Laser Scanning", *IEEE TGRS* 52(3):1739–1748. Multi-scan TLS on pine and spruce with destructive reference. Stem curve (diameter profile) accuracy ≈1 cm. Not an axis-shape metric — [UTU record](https://research.utu.fi/converis/portal/detail/Publication/458344017?lang=en_US)
- Stem quality assessment with TLS on ash (*Fraxinus*) with defects in two Irish stands. TLS taper, lean and sweep compared with manual measurement. Diameter estimation was better leaf-off. Sweep numbers not retrieved **[unverified]** — [Scand. J. For. Res. 30(7), 2015](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/02827581.2015.1043340)
- Hiraoka, Takahashi & Watanabe (2015), *J. Jpn. For. Soc.* 97:290–295. TLS on F1 progeny of *Cryptomeria japonica* plus trees. Each stem modelled as stacked horizontal circles **every 10 cm**. Two standardised straightness indices were checked against a field straightness index. Straightness had the highest ratio of additive genetic variance among the traits — [CiNii](https://cir.nii.ac.jp/crid/1390001205228540928?lang=en)

**Recent, axis-shape specific**
- Prendes, Canga, Ordoñez, Majada, **Acuna (University of the Sunshine Coast, QLD)** & Cabo (2022), "Automatic Assessment of Individual Stem Shape Parameters in Forest Stands from TLS Point Clouds: Application in *Pinus pinaster*", *Forests* 13(3):431. Data: FARO Focus3D, 24 scan positions, ~150 M points (≈26,300 pts/m²), slope >60%, 408 trees in a breeding trial, mean DBH 17.6 cm, h 10.2 m. Sections every **20 cm from 0.5 to 4.9 m**. Metrics: maximum sagitta, standardised max sagitta, sinuosity and PCA lean. Python implementation — [Forests 13:431](https://doi.org/10.3390/f13030431)
- Gorges, Huber, Sauter & Dormann (2021), "Curvature of Logs—Development of and Comparison between Different Calculation Approaches", *Forests* 12(7):857. 269 debarked 5 m logs (50 oak, 87 spruce, 132 Scots pine) scanned on a Microtec DiShape (laser triangulation, **360 points per slice, slices every ~5 mm**, ~1000 slices per 5 m log). Five algorithms compared with manual two-person bow-height measurement — [Freiburg PDF](https://www.biometrie.uni-freiburg.de/mitarbeiter/dormann/gorges-2021-forests.pdf); [MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/1999-4907/12/7/857)
- Pyörälä et al. (2019), "Assessing log geometry and wood quality in standing timber using terrestrial laser-scanning point clouds", *Forestry* 92(2):177–187 (doi 10.1093/forestry/cpy044). 52 Scots pines. TLS log geometry was compared with sawmill (log-scanner) measurements. Relative mean differences: top-end diameter 1.6%, volume −2.4%, taper −3.0%, **sweep 78%**. The authors note "remaining uncertainty in defining the sweep" and recommend mobile platforms for operational use (abstract-level only) — [UTU record](https://research.utu.fi/converis/portal/detail/Publication/458274175?lang=en_US)
- de Paula Pires, Lindgren, Persson & Holmgren (2026), "Stem crook detection in terrestrial laser scanning data using semi-empirical simulations", *Forestry* 99(3):cpag026. Scots pine, Leica RTC360, northern Sweden. Crooks were defined per Swedish log grading rules (Biometria 2025) as a bend with clear inward and outward bulge, annotated visually by staff trained by sawmill log graders. 65 annotated crooks; 53 of 310 test stems crooked — [Forestry 2026](https://academic.oup.com/forestry/article/99/3/cpag026/8607024)
- Windrim & Bryson (2020), "Detection, Segmentation, and Model Fitting of Individual Tree Stems from Airborne Laser Scanning of Forests Using Deep Learning", *Remote Sens.* 12(9):1469. **Helicopter Riegl VUX-1, ~300–700 pts/m², two *P. radiata* forests in NSW, Australia.** PointNet/3D-CNN stem segmentation, then a stem model with "radius, sweep, taper". Sweep is a model output but **not validated** against field sweep — [RS 12:1469](https://doi.org/10.3390/rs12091469)
- Larsen & Malmgren (2025), "Creating B-Spline Representations of Tree Stems from LiDAR Point Cloud Data", Lund University MSc (LUTFMA-3583-2025), for NFA Forestry Automation (harvester-mounted LiDAR). Density clustering for stem points, graph-based B-spline centerline, circle fits for radius. Synthetic data only (2,000 simulated clouds). Mean IoU 0.92 and volume RMSE 4.2% without odometry error; 0.83 and 8.2% with odometry error. Crookedness is the motivation, but no sweep metric is reported in the abstract — [LUP record](https://lup.lub.lu.se/student-papers/search/publication/9200220); [PDF](https://lup.lub.lu.se/student-papers/record/9200220/file/9200325.pdf)

**Australia / NZ applied (FWPA, Interpine, UTAS, Scion)**
- FWPA PNC464-1718 (University of Tasmania HIT Lab + Interpine NZ) built a VR cruising application. Cruisers assessed branching, **sweep** and features on ultra-dense helicopter LiDAR of *P. radiata* (Carabost SF, NSW; Riegl VUX-1LR flown at 30/60/90 m AGL; 60 m data used). Usability tests: 31 participants (pilot), then 17 experienced cruisers. Sweep was coded with Interpine PlotSafe codes — [FWPA PNC464 final report](https://fwpa.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2019/11/Amended_Final_Report_PNC464-1718.pdf)
- Same report: "LiDAR point cloud data acquired using normal (current) ALS scanning specifications does not provide point clouds sufficiently dense or sufficiently accurate for human visualisation and measurement in a VR environment, or for reliable application of automated measurements methods." A 2016 helicopter VUX-1UAV trial gave 475 pulses/m² (1,077 returns/m²) — [FWPA PNC464](https://fwpa.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2019/11/Amended_Final_Report_PNC464-1718.pdf)
- Earlier FWPA PNC326-1314 piloted recording sweep and other inventory features from dense ALS in VR, and PNC520-1920 continued "assessments of stem sweep and stem features ... in a Virtual Reality environment" (snippet-level) — [FWPA PNC326 report](https://fwpa.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2017/12/Amended_Final_Report_PNC326-1314-.pdf); [FWPA PNC546 report](https://fwpa.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/PNC546-2021-Final-Report_Characterising-native-forest-structure-from-co-incident-terrestrial-airborne-LiDAR.pdf)
- Scion (Hartley et al. 2022) backpack MLS pipeline for *P. radiata*: 90.3% individual-tree segmentation success and good DBH agreement. Some stems lacked enough points, and poor stem delineation caused DBH anomalies. No straightness output reported (secondary citation in an MPI document) — [MPI remote sensing document](https://www.mpi.govt.nz/dmsdocument/66294-Remote-Sensing-to-Measure-Carbon-Stock-Changes)
- FoSEM (2025, *Remote Sens.* 17(3):445), a LiDAR framework for stem extraction and cylinder modelling in radiata plantations: DBH RMSE 1.19 cm (4.67%), height RMSE 1.00 m. Figures show "flexure", but no validated straightness metric was found in the snippets — [FoSEM](https://doi.org/10.3390/rs17030445)
- Brazil: Emesent **Hovermap STX** (Wildcat SLAM) was flown below canopy over ~4,000 *E. cloeziana* / *E. saligna* trees at Itatinga (SP), with felled-tree validation every 2 m along the stem. Volume and diameter only; no straightness metric — [Scientia Agricola](https://www.scielo.br/j/sa/a/M8sM99fdRD5nvnGFG8tsB4M/?lang=en)

### Inferences
- The published state of the art for automated straightness from standing-tree LiDAR is essentially "fit section centers, then compute a chord-based bow/sagitta over a fixed length (and sometimes sinuosity and lean)". This is the same family as PS_LiDAR's chord-deviation approach. PS_LiDAR's multi-class, multi-window code emission (L vs S vs W, n_bows, kinks) is more elaborate than anything validated in the literature.
- Validated axis-shape accuracy only exists for (a) logs on sawmill scanners (Gorges), (b) high-density static TLS on short butt sections ≤5 m (Prendes), and (c) TLS vs sawmill sweep, where the result was poor (Pyörälä, 78% relative mean difference). For SLAM MLS (Hovermap, ZEB) the validated literature covers diameter profiles only, not sweep.
- Mauricio Acuna (University of the Sunshine Coast) co-authored the Prendes et al. sagitta/sinuosity method. He is a logical Queensland contact for validation collaboration.

- Could not verify any NZ University of Canterbury (Pont et al.) TLS straightness paper on radiata. Searches returned only a Canterbury thesis on personal laser scanning in a 9-year-old Kaingaroa trial ([UC thesis](https://ir.canterbury.ac.nz/server/api/core/bitstreams/09523541-31b7-41c6-a102-ddcefd7a5eb2/content)) with no straightness numbers extracted.
- Could not find Pitkänen et al. straightness work, Pollock, "Sun & Sweda", Marinelli, or a Krisanski/FSCT follow-up that computes sweep. FSCT outputs taper/DBH, and no sweep validation was found.
- Thies & Pfeifer 2004 numeric sweep/lean accuracy not retrieved. The 2015 Irish ash TLS sweep numbers were not retrieved either.
- A claim that a Scion field trial used a handheld scanner to measure "pruned height, stem straightness, taper and lean" appeared in a search summary without a traceable primary source **[unverified]**.

## Q2. Mathematical straightness metrics (chord/bow, mm/m, sagitta, sinuosity, curvature, segment sums, lean decomposition): definitions and behaviour with noisy centerlines

### Takeaway
Chord-based bow height (maximum distance from the centerline to the chord joining end-section centres) is the industry and literature standard. It is robust to centerline noise. Gorges et al. found center-of-mass and ellipse centerlines give identical rankings (ρ = 1), and moving-average smoothing from 50 to 200 mm changes the result by ≤4%. Pointwise mathematical (Frenet) curvature performed worst (ρ = 0.70 vs manual), even after 500 mm median+mean smoothing, and was judged "unusable for practical uses". Sinuosity (arc/chord) barely varies (mean 1.008 in *P. pinaster*) and has low discriminating power. Normalisation differs by jurisdiction: per length (cm/m), per mid-diameter, or per SED.

### Cited Findings

**Industry definitions (references for thresholds)**
- UK (Forestry Commission 1993 softwood sawlog classification): in a straight log the "bow must not exceed 1 cm for every 1 m in length and this in one plane and one direction only". Bow = maximum deviation of the actual centre line from the straight line joining the end centres. Visual standing-tree score 1–7 is based on straight log lengths in the bottom 6 m. Stand grades A–E by % of trees scoring 6–7, etc. — [FR TN021](https://cdn.forestresearch.gov.uk/2022/04/Assessing-the-stem-straightness-of-trees.pdf)
- Germany (RVR / RV WV): curvature = bow height B [mm] / (nominal length L_N [m] − reference-line offsets O [m]), i.e. cm/m. Austria (ÖNORM L 1021:2015) divides bow height by mid-log diameter instead — [Gorges et al. 2021](https://www.biometrie.uni-freiburg.de/mitarbeiter/dormann/gorges-2021-forests.pdf)
- South Australia (ForestrySA radiata sawlog spec SLSD002, 2016): "maximum sweep for all lengths shall not exceed SED/3", minimum SED 150 mm (snippet-level) — [ForestrySA](https://www.forestrysa.com.au/?p=2214)
- NZ: domestic grade tables express sweep classes as multiples/fractions of SED. Exact class limits were not retrieved **[unverified]** — [MPI NZ log grades page](https://www.mpi.govt.nz/forestry/forest-industry-and-workforce/forestry-wood-processing-data/new-zealand-log-grades-for-radiata-pine)
- NZ sawing studies: conversion was strongly affected when the sweep:diameter ratio exceeded **0.15** (logs averaging ~330 mm DBH) (snippet-level) — [Todoroki, NZJFS 25(2) 1995](https://www.scionresearch.com/__data/assets/pdf_file/0007/59596/NZJFS2521995TODOROKI246-255.pdf). At Mangatu, 20% of pruned butt logs had pronounced sweep (> D/4) (snippet-level) — [Cown et al., NZJFS 14(1) 1984](https://www.scionresearch.com/__data/assets/pdf_file/0004/59701/NZJFS1411984COWN109_123.pdf)
- Interpine PlotSafe dictionary as used in FWPA PNC464: **"6" straight; "2" moderate sweep; "S" gentle sweep over 4 m length; "L" gentle sweep over 6 m length; "1" excessive sweep; "W" wobble back and forth; "K" kink, sharp change of direction.** 2/S/L/6 are scored as *quality* errors and 1/W/K as *structural* errors in audits. Demerit weights by height: 0–12 m 6 (structural)/3 (quality); 12–20 m 4/2; >20 m 2/1 — [FWPA PNC464](https://fwpa.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2019/11/Amended_Final_Report_PNC464-1718.pdf)

**Gorges et al. 2021: the five algorithms and their behaviour** — [Gorges et al. 2021](https://www.biometrie.uni-freiburg.de/mitarbeiter/dormann/gorges-2021-forests.pdf)
- *Contour* (RV WV variant 1): 36 external reference lines at 10° spacing (more lines did not change results). Suspension points are averaged over ±10 cm at each end offset. Bow = max distance from contour to an external line. It picks up surface hollows and bark defects.
- *Segment* (RV WV variant 2, after Wilwerding): measuring points every 50 cm from mid-log. A bow height is computed per point, a diameter-dependent acceptable curvature is subtracted, and the excess is summed and normalised by mid diameter (%).
- *Centerline (center of mass)*: slice centroid polyline, smoothed with a moving average (5/10/15/20 cm tested). Max distance to the chord between offset end points.
- *Centerline (ellipse fit)*: least-squares circle/ellipse fit per slice (R package `conicfit`, Gama & Chernov), then the same as above.
- *Differential*: normalise by mean radius, smooth centres with a 500 mm median + averaging filter, compute κ = |f′×f″|/|f′|³ (forward difference for f′, central difference for f″), average κ over slices.
- Spearman ρ vs manual: ellipse-centerline 0.94, contour 0.94, center-of-mass 0.94, segment 0.79, **differential 0.70**. Center-of-mass vs ellipse centerline ρ = 1.0. Centerlines vs contour 0.97. Segment vs differential 0.61.
- Contour gave systematically higher values than centerline (mean +0.22 cm/m, median +0.19 cm/m), enough to change quality classes.
- End-offset sensitivity: changing butt/top offsets changed mean curvature by up to 15.7% (spruce), 10.3% (pine), 5.3% (oak). Recommendation: apply offsets at both ends (RV WV: 500 mm butt, 0 mm top) to suppress root flare and end defects.
- Smoothing sensitivity: moving-average window 50 → 200 mm reduced mean curvature only in the 2nd decimal (max 0.06 cm/m, 4%, oak). Weidenhiller & Golser found ≤4.6 mm bow-height difference (≈0.09 cm/m on a 5 m log).
- The differential approach was rejected: "by far the lowest correlation … strong effect of tree species … unusable for practical uses."
- Recommendation: adopt a centerline-based bow height for German grading. Center of mass is preferred for simplicity because it equals the ellipse fit in ranking. The segment approach's idea of integrating curvature along the log has merit, but different sweep *types* (Gjerdrum et al. 2004) need extra sweep characteristics.

**Prendes et al. 2022: sagitta, standardised sagitta, sinuosity, lean** — [Forests 13:431](https://doi.org/10.3390/f13030431)
- Sweep is "traditionally defined as the maximum sagitta per meter of the stem". Sagitta of each section = distance from section centre to the segment joining end-section centres. S_max and its height are recorded.
- **Gap-robust standardisation**: when a tree lacks sections at the top or bottom, the centres are assumed to lie on a circular arc of radius R = (S_max² + (H/2)²)/(2·S_max), where H is that tree's available height range. Standardised sagitta SS_max is then recomputed for the common height range (4.4 m). This makes trees with different visible lengths comparable.
- Sinuosity = polyline length through section centres / chord length (minimum 1). Observed: mean 1.008, SD 0.018, range 1.000–1.250. Operator−algorithm sinuosity differences clustered around −0.015.
- Lean = angle of the first principal component of section centres from vertical. Mean 4.0°, range 0.15–14.1°.
- SS_max: mean 10.4 cm (close to mean stem radius 8.8 cm), range 2–39 cm.

**Other metric families**
- Windrim & Bryson regularise the stem model with second differences of centre coordinates (x_c, y_c) as the "sweep" (curvature) term and of radius as the "taper" term. Weights W_xy = 1.0, W_r = 10.0, W_tip = 1.0, solved with Levenberg–Marquardt from RANSAC initial circles — [RS 12:1469](https://doi.org/10.3390/rs12091469)
- Pires et al. 2026 model crooks as single-directional (1dir) or double-directional with an inflection point (2dir) via trigonometric deformation equations. Parameter ranges: crook length 30–120 cm, lateral deviation 5–25 cm, inflection position 25–50%, inflection extension 50–100%. Literature crook deviations cited: 2.5–8.6 cm — [Forestry 2026](https://academic.oup.com/forestry/article/99/3/cpag026/8607024)
- A Springer 2023 paper citing Prendes notes that sinuosity values "averaged near to 1 … with little variation, possibly indicating that sinuosity has relatively low power to discriminate stem straightness" — [J. For. Res. 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11676-023-01600-x)

### Inferences
- **Chord-based bow is lean-invariant by construction.** The chord already absorbs a uniform tilt, so PCA lean and chord bow cleanly separate lean from sweep. This matches PS_LiDAR's design. Keep reporting lean (PCA of centres) separately, because a strongly leaning straight stem should not trigger sweep codes, and lean is itself a quality attribute (Prendes).
- **Why local curvature/turn angle is fragile.** With slice-center noise σ and spacing Δz, a second difference has noise ≈ √6·σ/Δz². For σ = 1 cm and Δz = 0.2 m this is ~0.6 m⁻¹, i.e. a radius of ~1.6 m, which is "kink-like". With a stencil of two 0.5 m chords, a single centre displaced by δ relative to its neighbours produces a turn angle of about 2δ/0.5 rad. A 15° (0.26 rad) kink is therefore triggered by δ ≈ 6–7 cm, an error that a partial-arc ellipse fit or one SLAM double-wall slice can easily produce. Gorges' empirical result (differential ρ = 0.70 even with 500 mm smoothing) supports moving kink detection to a model-based test: e.g. compare a two-segment piecewise-linear fit vs a smooth fit over ≥1 m (BIC/likelihood ratio), with minimum points and arc coverage per side.
- **Sinuosity is biased upward by noise.** Each segment length is √(Δz² + Δxy²), so noise adds ≈ Σ(δ²)/(2Δz) to the path length. With 0.2 m spacing and 1 cm noise per axis, that inflates sinuosity by roughly 0.25–0.5%, which is the same order as the real between-tree signal (mean 1.008). Use sinuosity only on a smoothed centerline, and do not use it as a primary class driver.
- **Counting direction reversals (n_bows) is noise-sensitive** for the same reason as curvature. Reversals should be counted on a smoothed lateral-offset profile with hysteresis, i.e. a minimum amplitude, e.g. ≥ SED/8 or ≥ 2σ of center noise, plus a minimum half-wavelength (e.g. ≥1–2 m). Raw sign changes should not be used. This mirrors how Pires et al. parameterise 2dir crooks with an explicit inflection point and extension.
- **Window boundary artifacts**: Gorges' offset analysis (up to 16% change) shows that end effects are first-order. Using offsets of ≥0.5 m at the butt (root flare, buttress, CSF/normalisation errors) and excluding the last partially-observed metres under the crown will likely reduce false S/W calls more than any smoothing change.
- Thresholds as SED fractions (PS_LiDAR) vs per-length (UK 1 cm/m; Germany cm/m) vs per-mid-diameter (Austria): the literature shows they produce different rankings. It is worth reporting the raw bow (mm), bow/length (cm/m) and bow/SED for each window so codes can be re-thresholded later against cruiser data.

### Gaps
- No paper found that uses Fourier or wavelet analysis of the lateral-offset profile, or counts inflection points, for standing-tree straightness from LiDAR. These remain untested ideas.
- Gjerdrum et al. (2004) sweep-type classification is cited by Gorges but was not retrieved.
- Exact NZ log-grade sweep class limits (d, 2d, …) and the HQP/Interpine definitions of codes "8", "3" and "X" were not found online. The FWPA dictionary uses "6" = straight and "2" = moderate, which differs from the user's 8/3 coding. This needs the HQP cruising manual.

## Q3. Centerline estimation robustness: circle vs ellipse vs cylinder fitting, smoothing, SLAM drift, occlusion gaps, slice thickness, point density

### Takeaway
On full-coverage data, the choice of centre estimator hardly matters (Gorges: ellipse fit ≡ center of mass, ρ = 1). On partial arcs from occluded MLS/ULS data the published best practice (FGI/Aalto) is arc-level processing. That means: detect arcs per short time window (1–3 s) to defeat SLAM drift, apply strict acceptance tests (residual SD, central angle > 0.6π, minimum points, radius bounds), use an algebraic "hyper-accurate" circle fit, use 0.2–0.4 m height bins, match arcs across bins, remove outliers by nearest neighbour, and fit a smoothing cubic spline. For gaps, model-based fills (regularised stem models, splines, circular-arc standardisation) are used rather than leaving holes.

### Cited Findings
- **FGI arc-based workflow (backpack, ZEB Horizon, under-canopy UAV)**, Hyyppä et al. 2020, *Remote Sens.* 12:3327 — [RS 12:3327](https://doi.org/10.3390/rs12203327):
  - 3D-scanner data are clustered by time window (**1 s** UAV, **3 s** ZEB Horizon, **2 s** backpack-A2). They are then binned in z into **0.4 m** height intervals above 1.0 m, clustered with DBSCAN per bin, and filtered with RANSAC circles (inlier ratio threshold 0.80 for ZEB; inlier distance 1.5 cm for backpack).
  - Circle fit: "hyper-accurate fit … approximating the geometric least squares circle fit with no bias by solving a generalized eigenvalue problem" (non-iterative).
  - Arc acceptance: radial-residual SD < 0.6 cm (backpack A1) / 0.75 cm (A2) / **1.5 cm (UAV and ZEB)**; **central angle > 0.6π rad**; ≥30 points (A1) or **≥50 points** (others); radius > 3–4 cm and < 40 cm.
  - Arc centres are clustered per tree (DBSCAN ε = 25 cm, minPts 5–10) and PCA gives the growth direction. Arcs are matched within **0.2 m** (A1) or **0.4 m** (A2/UAV/ZEB) bins by iterative arc matching in PCA coordinates, followed by nearest-neighbour outlier removal and a **smoothing cubic spline**.
  - Rationale: "positioning errors typically build up gradually over time and thus, they do not affect the structure of individual arcs". The arc approach eliminates SLAM drift distortion of stem models.
  - Results: stem-curve RMSE 2–15% depending on system and height. For the ZEB Horizon, bias/RMSE was ~0%/5% at breast height, rising to ~10%/15% by 7 m. UC-UAV bias was minimal at 3–4 m (flight height). Increasing range and branchiness with height are the likely causes.
- Hyyppä et al. 2020 (*ISPRS J.*), under-canopy UAV (Kaarta Stencil-1, VLP-16): stem curve RMSE 1.2 cm (5.0%) sparse and 1.4 cm (5.2%) obstructed. Stem detection 93% and 84% — [Aalto PDF](https://acris.aalto.fi/ws/portalfiles/portal/42219378/1_s2.0_S0924271620300915_main.pdf)
- Muhojoki et al. 2024, *Remote Sens.* 16:1721, six systems including **Hovermap (2020 version, VLP-16)**, ZEB Horizon, Deep Forestry UAV (Ouster OS0-32), in-house Ouster OS0-128 handhelds and helicopter Riegl VUX-1HA. Finite beam divergence inflates fitted radius roughly linearly with range. A bias-compensation model reduced |diameter bias| by 55–99%. VLP-16 systems were best: <10% RMSE for DBH and stem curve in easy plots (ZEB 6.6% stem curve / 4.5% DBH; Hovermap ≈7%). **Ouster OS0-based systems gave 15–25% RMSE** (higher beam divergence). Helicopter ALS approached the under-canopy accuracy but with much lower stem-detection completeness — [RS 16:1721](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/16/10/1721)
- Windrim & Bryson 2020 (helicopter ALS, radiata): PCA principal direction, then RANSAC circles per section, then robust least-squares refinement with a second-difference curvature penalty. The result is a single "tapering, sweeping cylinder" that "predict[s] sections of stem for which data is missing". The lower half of the stem adhered well to points, but upper-stem position was "more variable" because of stem/foliage confusion. A RANSAC-only geometric model "could not account for tree stems with a large amount of sweep". DBH error ≈15% — [RS 12:1469](https://doi.org/10.3390/rs12091469)
- Prendes et al. 2022 (TLS): sections at 20 cm. Missing sections were caused by insufficient points for circle fitting or by algorithm-flagged anomalies. Flagged sections went to a human operator, and outliers were removed before shape metrics. Operator-vs-operator section centres agreed within "less than 2% in 97%" of cases; operator vs algorithm was < 4 cm in 90% — [Forests 13:431](https://doi.org/10.3390/f13030431)
- Gorges et al. 2021 (log scanner): per-slice outlier filter. A point is implausible if |dist to centroid − mean of neighbours' dist| > 10%; it is replaced by the mean of 4 neighbours, with 3 iterations. Moving-average centerline smoothing of 50–200 mm had ≤4% effect on bow — [Gorges et al. 2021](https://www.biometrie.uni-freiburg.de/mitarbeiter/dormann/gorges-2021-forests.pdf)
- Pires et al. 2026 used a cubic smoothing spline (R `smooth.spline`) when generating synthetic crooks, and 3DFin for stem extraction (strip 0.25–5 m, stem search diameter 1 m) — [Forestry 2026](https://academic.oup.com/forestry/article/99/3/cpag026/8607024)
- Circle-fitting comparisons:
  - On UAV laser scanning, RANSAC gave better diameters than robust least trimmed squares (RLTS), though RMSE was high for both. A Hough transform was "not a suitable method for diameter fitting in ULS data" — [RS 12(8):1236](https://dx.doi.org/10.3390/rs12081236)
  - On TLS, the STEP algorithm handled occluded/noisy clouds better than CompuTree and SimpleTree but failed in the uppermost stem — [Forests 10(7):599](https://doi.org/10.3390/f10070599)
  - A stem-volume study reported that RANSAC correlated less with observed volume (R² = 0.68) than a circle fit (R² = 0.97), but the algebraic fit was biased by assuming a complete circle — [Forests 12(6):717](https://doi.org/10.3390/f12060717)
- Kalman filtering: a 2025 LiDAR–RGB forestry robot compared mean, median, EMA and Kalman temporal filters for stem radius. The Kalman filter was robust to noise and occlusion, while the mean and median filters had the lowest absolute errors (temporal tracking, not along-stem tracking) — [J. Robotics & Control 2025](https://journal.umy.ac.id/index.php/jrc/article/view/29228)

### Inferences
- **Ellipse fitting per 0.2 m slice is the most likely source of PS_LiDAR's center jitter on partial arcs.** Gorges' equivalence of ellipse and centroid holds only for 360° coverage. On arcs < ~0.6π (the FGI acceptance limit), a free 5-parameter ellipse is poorly constrained along the unobserved direction, and its centre can slide by centimetres. Recommended: fit circles (hyper/Taubin/Pratt, or geometric LS with IRLS/Huber), and use ellipses only when arc coverage exceeds ~240° and the axis ratio is plausible (e.g. < 1.2). Alternatively, fit a cylinder/cone over 0.4–1.0 m windows aligned with the local PCA axis, which also handles lean.
- **Add per-slice quality gates and propagate weights**, as FGI does: residual SD, central angle, n points, radius bounds and radius continuity with neighbours. Then use a *weighted* smoothing spline (or a Kalman/RTS smoother along z, with process noise tied to plausible curvature) over centres. Turn rejected slices into gaps instead of letting them through.
- **For SLAM data (Hovermap, ZEB), consider the time-window arc trick.** Fit arcs per GPS-time chunk (1–3 s) so that drift between passes does not create double walls, which make a single slice-wide fit wander. This addresses "jitter of slice centers" at its root rather than by smoothing.
- **Occlusion gaps**: (i) interpolate centres across gaps with the spline/regularised model (Windrim-style second-difference penalty), but flag interpolated spans and forbid kink or W calls inside them; (ii) standardise bow over the observed length using the circular-arc formula (Prendes) when comparing to SED-based thresholds; (iii) stop sweep classification where the arc/point-quality criteria fail persistently, as the upper stem is where every MLS study reports bias rising.
- Slice thickness: published bins are 0.2 m (backpack 2D scan-lines, Prendes TLS sections at 0.2 m spacing) to 0.4 m (VLP-16 class SLAM/ULS). Moving PS_LiDAR from 0.2 m to 0.3–0.4 m bins for Hovermap/ZEB data, or using overlapping 0.4 m windows stepped at 0.2 m, should cut center noise by ~√2 with negligible loss of sweep resolution. Sweep classes operate at 4–6 m scales (S over 4 m, L over 6 m), and crooks span 0.3–1.2 m.
- Beam-divergence bias (Muhojoki) inflates radius roughly symmetrically, so it should affect centres much less than diameters. But it matters for SED-normalised thresholds: an over-estimated SED makes sweep classes too lenient. Check whether SED is taken from fitted radii that are biased at range.

### Gaps
- No study found that quantifies slice-centre error versus slice thickness, arc coverage, or point density for *centerline* accuracy (only for diameter).
- No published along-stem Kalman/RTS smoother for LiDAR stem centerlines was found. It would be a novel but straightforward component.
- Minimum point density for reliable sweep: no explicit value was found. PNC464 only states that normal ALS is insufficient and that ultra-dense helicopter ULS (~475 pulses/m²) was still insufficient for humans to see wobble.

## Q4. Reported accuracies and validation against field, harvester or sawmill measurements; inter-operator variability

### Takeaway
Accuracy figures for axis shape are sparse and mostly modest. On logs, centerline bow correlates ρ = 0.94 with manual measurement (Gorges). On standing TLS butt sections, algorithm sagitta is within 10 cm of operator sagitta in 92% of trees (Prendes). TLS sweep vs sawmill sweep had a 78% relative mean difference (Pyörälä). Automated crook detection reached F1 ≈ 0.45 at IoU 0.5, with recall > 0.8 for severe crooks (Pires). Field visual straightness classes are themselves unreliable: in Prendes, field classes did not differ significantly in TLS sagitta, and in PNC464 VR cruisers missed wobble 13 times out of 17. Sawmill-scanner certification tolerances (RV WV: mean ≤0.1 cm/m, >0.3 cm/m in <10% of logs) are a concrete accuracy target.

### Cited Findings
- **Gorges et al. 2021 (log scanner vs manual):** the contour algorithm exceeded manual measurements in 72.6% (pine) and 90.4% (spruce) of logs. Mean difference 0.09 cm/m; 7.8% of logs differed by >0.3 cm/m. RV WV certification limits: mean deviation ≤0.1 cm/m, and >0.3 cm/m deviation in <10% of logs. Rank correlations vs manual: centerline 0.94, contour 0.94, segment 0.79, differential 0.70. Mean centerline curvature (B500_T0 offsets): spruce 0.78 cm/m, pine 1.04 cm/m, oak 1.65 cm/m. The manual reference itself "is not particularly accurate" (sample biased to visibly curved logs: 179 of 219 conifer logs) — [Gorges et al. 2021](https://www.biometrie.uni-freiburg.de/mitarbeiter/dormann/gorges-2021-forests.pdf)
- **Prendes et al. 2022 (TLS *P. pinaster*, 385 of 408 trees processed):**
  - Op1−Op2 lean differences < 0.25° in 99% of trees; Op−Alg lean within ±0.5° in 95%.
  - SS_max differences: Op1−Op2 < 5 cm in 99%; **Op−Alg < 10 cm in 92%**, with TLS slightly larger than operators.
  - Field visual classes (3 straightness and 3 lean categories) had mean SS_max 9.5 / 13.9 / 14.4 cm. A Tukey–Kramer test (α = 0.05) found **no significant difference among straightness classes**. Lean class 1 differed from classes 2–3 only.
  - The authors conclude visual categories involve "a clear misclassification" — [Forests 13:431](https://doi.org/10.3390/f13030431)
- **Pyörälä et al. 2019 (TLS vs sawmill, Scots pine, 52 trees):** sweep relative mean difference 78% (vs 1.6% top diameter, −2.4% volume, −3.0% taper) — [UTU record](https://research.utu.fi/converis/portal/detail/Publication/458274175?lang=en_US)
- **Pires et al. 2026 (TLS crook detection, Scots pine, independent site):**
  - F1 > 0.6 for IoU < 0.3; at IoU 0.5, F1 ≈ 0.45 (recall 0.55, precision 0.35); mAP@50–95 = 0.24.
  - Recall for severe crooks > 0.80 at IoU 0.5; mild crooks degraded quickly.
  - True positives mostly at 3–8 m; commissions concentrated above 10 m (branches, sparse points); omissions mostly below 4 m (irregular butt).
  - Reference was visual annotation of TLS data, not physical crook measurements — [Forestry 2026](https://academic.oup.com/forestry/article/99/3/cpag026/8607024)
- **FWPA PNC464 (VR cruising on helicopter ULS of radiata, 17 cruisers, 6 trees):**
  - Heights mostly within ±1 m tolerance; height SD between participants 0.15–0.29 m.
  - Manual VR DBH circles biased high; between-participant SD 50–65 mm.
  - For sweep, "wobble" below 12 m was missed by 13 of 17 participants. Missed "1" (excessive sweep) above 20 m was also a common error.
  - Only 44% agreed sweep estimation was easy (44% neutral, 12% disagree/strongly disagree).
  - Cause stated: insufficient point density on the stem where the profile changes, plus view-dependent perception without surface rendering — [FWPA PNC464](https://fwpa.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2019/11/Amended_Final_Report_PNC464-1718.pdf)
- **UK visual scoring vs felled measurement:** 81% of trees within ±1 of the score measured after felling (1–7 scale, bottom 6 m). The note stresses training, checking and refresher courses for consistency between surveyors — [FR TN021](https://cdn.forestresearch.gov.uk/2022/04/Assessing-the-stem-straightness-of-trees.pdf)
- **Stem-curve (diameter-profile) accuracies, for context only**:
  - TLS ≈1 cm ([Liang et al. 2014](https://research.utu.fi/converis/portal/detail/Publication/458344017?lang=en_US)).
  - Under-canopy UAV 1.2–1.4 cm, 5.0–5.2% ([Hyyppä et al. 2020 ISPRS](https://acris.aalto.fi/ws/portalfiles/portal/42219378/1_s2.0_S0924271620300915_main.pdf)).
  - Backpack, ZEB and UAV 2–15% by height ([Hyyppä et al. 2020 RS](https://doi.org/10.3390/rs12203327)).
  - Hovermap/ZEB ≈7% and Ouster-based 15–25% ([Muhojoki et al. 2024](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/16/10/1721)).
  - Average cross-section RMSE: TLS 1.03 cm, handheld MLS 1.26 cm, close-range photogrammetry 1.90 cm (Hunčaga et al. 2020, *Remote Sens.*, snippet-level; I inferred the URL below from the search listing and did not open it) — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/4c844602ae1547e8ace1fb996920090f)
  - Harvester-integrated MLS: DBH RMSE 3.2 cm, stem curve 3.6 cm — [RS 16:3292](https://dx.doi.org/10.3390/rs16173292)
- Breeding context: straightness is routinely a subjective score. Examples: Scion radiata 1–9 (1 = very crooked) (snippet-level, [Scion NZJFS 27(2)](https://www.scionresearch.com/__data/assets/pdf_file/0016/17107/NZJFS2721997JAYAWICKRAMA126-141.pdf)); loblolly 1–6 ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13334174/)); FWPA breeding reports define sweep as maximum deviation of log axis from a straight line in mm/m with straightness scored 1–6 (snippet-level).
  - A UAV-LiDAR radiata phenotyping study across 11 genetic trials used height/volume, not straightness — [Research Square](https://www.researchsquare.com/article/rs-10234920/v1)
  - A Scots pine LiDAR genetic-trial paper states stem quality/defects "could not yet be obtained" from LiDAR — [Ann. For. Sci. 2025](https://annforsci.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s13595-025-01283-w)

### Inferences
- The field cruiser's sweep codes are a noisy reference. Prendes found no significant separation of visual straightness classes by measured sagitta, UK visual scoring is within ±1 class only 81% of the time, and VR cruisers mostly missed wobble. Validation of PS_LiDAR should therefore (a) use a measured reference on a subset (felled-tree or log-scanner bow, or a pole-and-tape bow on butt logs) and (b) report agreement with cruiser codes as Cohen's κ / weighted κ, including a cruiser-vs-cruiser κ baseline from repeated plots. Raw % agreement alone is not enough.
- A realistic target for log-scale bow accuracy, borrowed from sawmill certification, is a mean bias ≤ 0.1 cm/m with < 10% of logs off by > 0.3 cm/m. For a 6 m log that is ≤6 mm mean bias and > 18 mm error in < 10% of cases. That is far tighter than SED/8 for typical plantation SEDs (e.g. SED 200 mm gives 25 mm). This suggests PS_LiDAR's lowest threshold (SED/8) is close to what MLS centerline noise can resolve, and borderline 8-vs-S/L decisions will be noise-dominated.
- Height dependence: every MLS/ULS study shows errors growing with height, and Pires shows false crooks concentrated above 10 m. Sweep codes in the crown zone should carry lower confidence or be suppressed, consistent with the PlotSafe audit weighting that discounts > 20 m errors.

### Gaps
- No study found that compares LiDAR-derived sweep classes with Australian/NZ field cruiser codes (Interpine/HQP) or with harvester-recorded sweep. Harvester heads generally do not log sweep, and no validation of that was found.
- Cavalheiro et al. 2026 (*J. For. Res.*, TLS loblolly progeny test, NC) is confirmed to report DBH heritability: field h² 0.32, raw TLS 0.17, cleaned TLS 0.27, GCA correlation 0.92 ([JFR](https://journal.hep.com.cn/jfr/EN/10.1007/s11676-026-02007-0)). A search summary claiming its straightness "correlated strongly with trained forester ratings" could **not** be verified.
- No published inter-cruiser κ for Interpine/HQP sweep codes was found.

## Q5. Machine-learning / deep-learning approaches for stem quality or straightness from point clouds, and labelled datasets

### Takeaway
Direct DL classification of straightness or log grade from standing-tree point clouds is almost absent. The one clear example is Pires et al. 2026: YOLOv8s on 2D rasterised 2 m stem sections, trained on 14,000 *synthetically simulated* crooks and tested on real TLS. DL is otherwise used upstream (stem/wood segmentation: PointNet/PointNet++, 3D-CNN), with straightness computed geometrically afterwards. No public dataset with straightness or sweep labels was found. Simulation-based training data (deforming real straight stems) is the documented workaround.

### Cited Findings
- **Pires et al. 2026** — [Forestry 2026](https://academic.oup.com/forestry/article/99/3/cpag026/8607024):
  - Deformations were applied along X only to branch-free segmented straight stems. 50 simulations per model stem, 14,000 synthetic crooks.
  - Stems were cut into **2 m sections with 0.5 m overlap**. Each section was rotated 0–160° in 20° steps (9 views) and rasterised to binary 192×96 images.
  - Training: YOLOv8s, 15 epochs, batch 128, dropout 0.25, 70/30 split keeping all views of a crook together. At inference, boxes are merged across views.
  - Stated limitations: X-only deformation, a 5 cm minimum deviation (smaller crooks missed), deviation not scaled to diameter, single species and site, and sim-to-real domain shift.
- **Windrim & Bryson 2020**: R-CNN/3D-CNN tree detection, then PointNet-based stem-point segmentation (foliage / lower stem / upper stem), then a regularised geometric stem model with sweep. Helicopter ALS over NSW radiata. Stem reconstruction error fell to ~9% when ground-truth stem labels were used, showing that segmentation errors dominate — [RS 12:1469](https://doi.org/10.3390/rs12091469)
- A machine-learning classifier of bark-surface defects (branches, scars, epicormics) on TLS for oak, beech, fir and pine reached an average F1 0.86 (snippet-level). It targets defects linked to grading rules, not sweep — [AGRIS record](https://agris.fao.org/search/ar/records/65df83510f3e94b9e5d952c9)
- A 2024 review of ML for log scaling and grading found DL performed better for log-pile scaling and log segmentation. Most log *side-grading* studies used laser-scanned point clouds of felled logs — [Forests 15(7):1243](https://www.mdpi.com/1999-4907/15/7/1243)
- Stereo vision + DL phenotyping of pine architecture (stem straightness, branch angle) was proposed because visual grading in progeny trials is low-throughput — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/353105826_Phenotyping_of_Pine_Tree_Architecture_with_Stereo_Vision_and_Deep_Learning)
- Datasets:
  - FOR-instance (UAV LiDAR benchmark, includes an 18–20-year-old Scion radiata plantation) has instance/semantic labels only, and stem separation is poor in the upper crown — [arXiv 2309.01279](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.01279)
  - An IEEE DataPort handheld SLAM-LiDAR radiata stem dataset (per-tree annotations and splits; lists "stem flexure" as a use case) has no straightness labels verified — [IEEE DataPort](https://ieee-dataport.org/documents/3d-lidar-point-clouds-and-ground-truth-dataset-forest-stem-modeling)
  - The UEF Scots pine TLS datasets (230 trees, stem size/shape) have no straightness labels verified — [UEF dataset](https://erepo.uef.fi/handle/123456789/33136)
- The Lund thesis (2025) trained and evaluated only on simulated harvester scans (2,000 clouds, with and without odometry error), showing that simulation is the default for curvature ground truth — [LUP](https://lup.lub.lu.se/student-papers/search/publication/9200220)

### Inferences
- For PS_LiDAR, a hybrid approach is more defensible than end-to-end DL given the lack of labelled data. Keep the geometric centerline and chord metrics, but replace the hand-tuned decision tree (8/L/S/3/1/X/W/K) with a small classifier trained on per-window features: bow amplitude/SED, bow/length, window length of the maximum, number of hysteresis reversals, max turn angle on smoothed centerline, residual of a piecewise-linear fit, data coverage, slice-quality flags and height. Train it on cruiser codes plus a measured subset, and use ordinal losses because sweep severity is ordinal. Pires' simulation recipe (deform real straight stems, render with realistic occlusion) can generate synthetic L/S/W/K examples to stress-test thresholds before field labels exist.
- The Pires height pattern (false positives above 10 m, misses below 4 m) and Windrim's segmentation-dominated error both indicate that stem/branch separation quality, not the curvature metric, is the main accuracy limiter in the crown zone.

### Gaps
- No PointNet, graph-network or CNN-on-unwrapped-stem model was found that predicts sweep class or sawlog grade directly from standing-tree point clouds and is validated against field or sawmill data.
- No public dataset with sweep, crook or straightness labels for standing trees was found, for *Pinus* (PHYB, slash, Caribbean, radiata) or any other genus.
